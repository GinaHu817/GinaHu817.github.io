IQuest-Q1: The Open Model That Did Every Stage of Training (and Then Helped Build Its Own Successor)

A brand-new open-weight agent model that doesn't just polish the last step. It shows its work from pre-training to RL, measures how far it could safely scale, and even helps debug its own training data.
October 2026  ·  11 min read

So far on this blog I've gone from the paper that invented the Transformer to Stanford's giant report on foundation models. This time I wanted something fresh off the press: IQuest-Q1: Advancing Foundation Capabilities for Agentic CLI Systems, from IQuest Research, posted to arXiv on October 22, 2026. It's an open-weight model built to work as an agent in the command line: reading files, running code, using tools, and finishing long, multi-step jobs.

Here's why it grabbed me. A lot of new-model reports mostly talk about post-training, the final polish. This one walks through the whole pipeline: pre-training, mid-training, and post-training (SFT, RL, distillation, and model merging). It's like getting the entire recipe instead of just a photo of the cake.

04 RL & MOPDDomain RL, 4 experts → 1 student
05 MergingCombine models into IQuest-Q1

The Model at a Glance

IQuest-Q1 is a sparse Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) model. That's the trick where a model is huge on paper, but each token only wakes up a small team of "experts" inside it.

320B

15B

8 / 256

29T

Think of it like a hospital with 256 specialists per floor. You don't see all of them for a sore throat. A router sends you to the 8 who matter. You get the knowledge of a giant model at roughly the running cost of a much smaller one.

How does it stack up?

The paper tests it on a huge list of coding and general-agent benchmarks. My honest read: it's competitive with the other open models it's compared against, and on most benchmarks it still trails the two big proprietary models, Claude Opus 5 and GPT-5.6 Sol. But for a model with only 15B active parameters, that's a pretty moderate size to be in that conversation at all. And there are a few fun upsets:

The part that really surprised me: it helps build its successor

IQuest-Q1 takes part in an iterative model-development loop. An agent built on the model looks for its own weaknesses, proposes a fix, implements it, and evaluates the result, while humans review key decisions. The authors call it human-on-the-loop, a practical path toward recursive self-improvement.

Their example is wonderfully concrete. The model was great with familiar tool-call formats but stumbled on unfamiliar ones, producing 35 tool-call errors in one long-horizon task. The agent traced the failures across 6,054 tool calls and found that the training data was heavily biased toward one tool-call schema, and some records even had tool definitions and call parameters that didn't match (a preprocessing bug). It proposed cleaning those records and mixing in more diverse schemas. Humans approved, and the fix ran as a training job of 2.35B tokens and 938.7 GPU hours, then got checked on both new and familiar schemas for regressions.

A model debugging its own training data is a little bit "Inception." But notice that humans still approve the expensive runs. The paper is clear that human supervision matters here.

Part 1: Pre-Training, Where the Unusual Stuff Lives

📏 How big can you go before training breaks?

This was my favorite idea in the whole paper. When you train a model, there are two learning rates you care about:

  • the optimal learning rate (ηopt): the one that trains most efficiently on a fixed budget, and
  • the safe learning rate (ηsafe): the largest one that still trains stably. They find it by grid search, sorting runs into stable, warning (loss spikes, then recovers), and collapsed (loss spikes and stays high).

As long as the safe rate sits above the optimal rate, you have headroom. They measure it as:

HN = log( ηsafe(N) / ηopt(N) )

At IQuest-Q1's own scale (15B active), the safe learning rate is about 2× the optimal one. Plenty of safety margin. Then the clever part: they plot both curves across model sizes and extrapolate to where the two lines meet. They call that crossing point the Critical Model Size, and it lands at about 572B activated parameters (about 12T total). In other words, this recipe should still scale far beyond the model they actually trained.

📌 Honestly, I've never seen a paper do this before. Most reports tell you "we trained a big model and it was fine." This one tells you how much bigger it could get before things break. The idea is inspired by the "critical batch size" concept, but applied to model size.

Bonus stat: in a controlled comparison against a Qwen3-30B-A3B-style design trained on identical data, IQuest-Q1's architecture showed 1.93× higher scaling efficiency, reaching the same training loss with only about 52% of the compute.

🧱 Architecture tweaks compared with a "standard" LLM

Grouped-Query Attention (GQA). The model has 48 query heads but only 8 key/value heads, so every 6 query heads share the same K and V. Since the KV cache stores keys and values per KV head, this makes it 6× smaller than giving every query head its own. It also mixes attention types: after the first two layers (and before the last two), which use full attention, the stack repeats three sliding-window layers (window of 4,096 tokens) followed by one full-attention layer.

Stable SplitNorm. Remember the residual connections from my Foundation Models post? Here's a twist on them. In the usual Pre-Norm setup, the residual stream itself is never normalized, so its variance can keep growing with depth, and later layers end up contributing relatively less. Deep layers get underused. SplitNorm splits the strategy between the two sublayers:

z = RMSNorm(x)
h = z + Attention(z)   ← the stream entering attention is normalized
y = h + FFN(RMSNorm(h))  ← the stream around the FFN stays unnormalized

So it normalizes selected paths while keeping an identity shortcut around the FFN. The goal is to balance using all that depth (the Post-Norm strength) with stable optimization (the Pre-Norm strength). The "Stable" part: scaling this up naively got unstable, so they also add an RMSNorm on the attention output to stop activations from exploding, and a SandwichNorm on the very first layer.

QKNorm Clip. Normalizing the queries and keys keeps attention scores from blowing up. The "clip" part caps the learnable scale parameters of that normalization at 10, so they can't quietly grow huge either.

Query-aware sink attention. Softmax forces attention weights to sum to 1. So a token with nothing useful to look at still has to dump its attention somewhere, like a student who must hand in an answer even when they don't know it. The sink is a special destination for that leftover attention. In the architecture diagram it has a key but a zero value, so attention dumped there adds nothing to the output.

Part 2: Mid-Training, the Bridge to Being an Agent

Mid-training sits between general pre-training and agent-specific post-training. Two ideas stood out:

  • Progressive context extension. Train on shorter contexts first, then longer: 32K → 128K → 512K tokens. Like learning to read paragraphs before novels.
  • Token-level loss masking. Normally you decide what to learn from by role (e.g. train on everything the assistant said). Here they mark individual tokens, so supervision can target exactly the spans that matter, with finer control.

They also built more than one million executable, containerized task instances from code repositories, ran an agent on each, and kept the valid, complete trajectories as training data.

How do you grade a model that isn't finished yet?

They use bits-per-byte (BPB) on held-out agent trajectories. Lower is better: a low BPB means the model assigns higher likelihood to what competent agents actually did. Nice bonus: it doesn't require the model to generate anything, so it's a cheap, fine-grained signal.

They also break long agent tasks into atomic abilities and test each one, which helps pinpoint exactly which skill is missing.

Part 3: Post-Training, Where It Learns to Behave Like an Agent

🕵️ Stopping the model from cheating

Reward hacking is when a model finds a shortcut to the reward instead of actually solving the task. Picture an agent asked to fix a bug that just digs the original fix out of the Git history. To stop this, the paper uses three layers of protection plus task-specific safeguards:

  1. Environment preparation. Clean the Git history, remove files that could reveal solutions, or build fresh environments with only the required materials.
  2. Execution-time checks. Network restrictions, intercepting tool calls, and blocking rules developed from observed hacking behavior. Blocked actions get explicit feedback, so the agent can keep working the honest way.
  3. Trajectory auditing. Rule-based checks plus rubric-guided LLM judges review the commands and outputs, looking for things like grabbing leaked fixes, peeking at hidden tests, or editing evaluation scripts.
  4. Task-specific safeguards. For example, for SWE-like tasks they keep Git history only up to the base commit and use hooks in Claude Code, Codex, and OpenCode to block attempts to fetch existing solutions.
My favorite detail: the blocking rules come from catching the model cheating in the first place. Every trick it tries becomes a new rule. It's an arms race, and they keep score.

🏋️ Environment-centered reinforcement learning

Real users run agents inside different "harnesses" (Claude Code, Codex, and different versions of each), and each one has its own tools and quirks. Instead of training one model per harness, the team trains one shared policy on trajectories collected from diverse harness configurations. That's how you get a model that doesn't panic when you move it to a new tool. I think this is amazing.

They're also careful about blame. When an episode fails, mistakes caused by the policy become learning signals, but faults in the environment or harness get masked out, and samples with evaluator faults aren't used at all. The model only gets punished for its own mistakes. Fair.

🎓 MOPD: four teachers, one student

Instead of asking one model to learn every skill alone, they train expert models for different capabilities and then use them to teach a single student. That's Multi-Teacher On-Policy Distillation:

The per-token score is basically clip(log(p_teacher / p_student), −c, c). If the teacher likes a token more than the student does, the score is positive and the student leans toward it. The clip keeps any single token from yanking the update too hard. And one funny detail: rollouts where the student gets stuck repeating itself get a mask of 0, so they don't count at all.

Limitations

  • Text only. No native image, audio, or video input.
  • Check its work. Explanations and code can be wrong, so review changes and verify them with real tests.
  • Real CLI work is messy. The model may miss constraints, repeat failed attempts, or leave issues unresolved, so humans still need to supervise.

Cheat Sheet: Concepts Worth Knowing

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) A model with many "expert" sub-networks, where a router activates only a few per token. Lots of total parameters, much less compute per token.
Atomic abilities Small, individually measurable skills an AI agent needs to complete a larger, multi-step task.
Bits-per-byte (BPB) How "surprised" the model is by real text, measured per byte. Lower = it finds good agent behavior more likely.
GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization) A reinforcement learning algorithm that improves an LLM by comparing the rewards of multiple attempts at the same task, then raising the probability of the behaviors that did better than the group average. In IQuest-Q1, the repeated attempts are compared under the same harness and evaluator version.
MOPD (Multi-Teacher On-Policy Distillation) Instead of one model learning every skill on its own, train several expert models for different capabilities, then use those experts to teach a single student model, scoring the student's own outputs.
Muon The optimizer IQuest-Q1 uses for most of its weights (token embeddings, the LM head, normalization parameters, and MoE routers use Adam instead). It's on my to-read list.
Reward hacking When a model exploits loopholes (leaked answers, weak tests) to get rewarded without actually solving the task.

TL;DR

  • IQuest-Q1 is an open-weight MoE agent model for the command line: 320B total parameters, 15B active per token.
  • Unlike many releases, the paper covers the whole pipeline: pre-training, mid-training, SFT, RL, MOPD, and model merging.
  • The coolest idea: measuring how far the training recipe can safely scale. The safe learning rate is about 2× the optimal one at 15B active, and the extrapolated limit is about 572B active parameters.
  • Architecture tricks: GQA (6 query heads per KV head), Stable SplitNorm, QKNorm Clip at 10, and sink attention for "nowhere to look" tokens.
  • Post-training fights reward hacking on several fronts, trains one policy across many harnesses, and distills four frozen experts into one student.
  • It's competitive with other open models and mostly behind Claude Opus 5 and GPT-5.6 Sol, with a few wins. It's also helping build its own successor, with humans on the loop.
